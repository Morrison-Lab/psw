This chapter lists the book’s main rules as a checklist. Use the checklist when you revise a draft: read the draft once for each group of rules, and fix each passage that breaks a rule. Each rule is stated in a few words here. Follow its link for the reasons and the exceptions.

1 Terms and notation

  • Define every technical term explicitly, before its first use, and follow the definition with examples and counterexamples.

  • Give each mathematical statement an example that applies it.

  • Prefer interval notation to a double inequality: \(x \in (a, b)\) rather than \(a < x < b\).

  • Abstract each sub-expression you are not manipulating to a symbol, such as \(\mu\) for \(\E{X}\).

  • Do one operation per step in a derivation, and justify each step.

  • Give applying a definition its own step; never skip it.

  • Show why you need a result before you derive it, and color each result where it is used.

  • Put one equals sign on each line: split a chain such as \(a = b = c\) into the rows of an aligned display.

  • Distinguish a function from its value.

  • Distinguish a random variable from its value: \(Y_i\) before the data, \(y_i\) after, and call each a “variable” or a “value” in the words too.

  • Use the notation that matches a quantity’s name, and keep the pairing the same everywhere.

  • Write each symbol through a shared macro.

  • Write out notational shorthands, such as the limits of a sum.

  • Put words between formulas, and start a sentence with a word, not a symbol.

  • Introduce a theorem with a complete sentence, and state it so that it stands alone.

  • Spell out a number used as an adjective, and write the numeral for a number used as a name.

2 Claims and evidence

  • Support every claim with a citation or with data.
  • Do not substitute flippancy for support, such as calling a step “trivial”.
  • Cite a relevant source that is trustworthy.
  • Copy exact wording with caution, even from your own earlier work.
  • Cite a source once per passage.
  • Link liberally to the sources and tools that you mention.
  • Quote the original rather than a source that quotes it.
  • Put the content first: name the topic, not its author, in headings and sentences.

3 Papers

  • Write to clarify your thinking: write each question or idea down as it comes.
  • Follow the standard structure of a research paper.
  • Organize the Results section to fit the design of the study.
  • Label variables in plain words in figures and tables.
  • Use descriptive subsection headings.
  • Avoid forward references: move the content ahead of the sentence that uses it.
  • Put each link to a video next to the text that it supports.
  • Follow the journal’s conventions for a manuscript, and check the submission checklist before you submit.

4 Words

  • Prefer plain words to Latin-derived ones where a plain word says the same thing.
  • Avoid vague and metaphorical language.
  • Follow an abstract statement with an example.
  • Replace the word “thing” with the object, quantity, step, or idea that it stands for.
  • Give a technical word one meaning.
  • Minimize unnecessary jargon.
  • Edit out clusters of clichéd words and sentence shapes, which make prose read as generic or machine-drafted.
  • Say what something is before what it is not.

5 Writing for every reader

  • Use literal language.
  • Use common words, and define technical ones.
  • Say when a common word has a technical meaning, such as “significant” or “bias”.
  • Spell out abbreviations.
  • Write positive statements, and avoid double negatives.
  • Rewrite noun strings and hidden verbs.
  • Put one idea in each sentence.
  • State every step and every point.
  • Say what numbers and symbols mean.
  • Use examples that every reader knows.
  • Keep a predictable structure.
  • Present ideas in more than one form.
  • Test your writing with a reader.

6 Sentences

  • Keep relative pronouns such as “that” and “which”.
  • Follow “this” or “that” with the noun that it refers to.
  • Follow a quantifier such as “both” or “each” with its noun.
  • Avoid garden-path sentences.
  • Name the object of a relational noun.
  • Lead with the subject.
  • Make every word tell: cut words that carry no meaning, but keep detail that does.
  • Limit complex sentence structures.
  • Do not rule out readings that no reader would make.
  • Write about the subject, not about the text.
  • Put a list of three or more phrases in bullet points.
  • Write to communicate, not to impress: use the plainest form that still carries the full meaning.

7 Teaching materials

  • Build the lecture from exercises and solutions.
  • Ask the question before giving the definition.
  • Present a derivation as an exercise and solution, then the theorem and its proof.
  • Give each defined term its own div.
  • State the notation and its alternatives.
  • Lead with the simplest correct form of a definition.
  • Reuse one written version of each result.
  • Use real data and compute every number.
  • Show each idea applied and in a figure.
  • Write labs in both R and Python that run on any computer.
  • Make figures accessible.
  • Structure slides for the reader.
  • Credit another course’s material in a collapsed “Source” callout, not in running prose.
  • Do not formally acknowledge material borrowed from your own work, and remove any existing credit to your own work, but keep what that work itself credits.

8 Software text

  • Say what changed and why in a pull request or commit message.
  • Comment why, not what.
  • Write release notes by their effect on the user.

9 Thinking

  • Write the question down first.
  • Send a written question that gives the reader the context to answer it.
  • Draft the plan before you run the analysis.
